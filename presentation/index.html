The problem

Students confuse feeling ready with being ready

1

Hours aren't learning

Rereading, highlighting and watching videos feel productive. They're among the least effective ways to study.

2

Recognizing isn't knowing

Seeing the right answer and thinking "I knew that" is not the same as being able to produce it on your own.

3

Blind spots stay hidden

The most dangerous gap is what you're sure you know and don't. You'll never review it, because you think it's done.

The exam doesn't ask what you saw. It asks why.

Students put in the hours. The problem is that the methods that feel best (rereading, highlighting, rewatching) build familiarity, not knowledge. And the gaps they're confident about never get reviewed.

The problem with using AI

AI made the shortcut free

Students turned to AI to study faster. Now it answers for them, and the effort that builds memory goes away.

92%

HEPI Student Generative AI Survey, 2025
−17%

Bastani et al., PNAS, 2025
83%

MIT Media Lab, 2025 (preprint)

In the same PNAS study, a tutor with guardrails that didn't hand over answers removed the harm.

The usual fix for bad studying is now AI, and it makes the problem worse: it does the part that creates learning. The key line is the last one. The issue isn't AI itself, it's AI with no guardrails, and that's where we come in.

An analogy

Learning is crossing a desert

The crossingSlow and tiring, and it's what changes you. Every step you take yourself stays with you.

The shortcutYou get there on time but you never crossed. You spend the hours and keep nothing.

AI shouldn't carry you across. It should walk next to you.

If someone flies you over the desert, you arrive but you can't cross it again on your own. Skipping the effort doesn't save time: you spend the time and keep nothing. The role we want for AI is a guide that walks with you.

Learning science has a clear ranking. Rereading and highlighting rate low. Practice testing (retrieval) rates high. Self-explanation goes further: it forces you to connect the idea and shows the exact gap. Most study apps live at recognition. We built for explanation.

Our principle

If I can't explain it, I don't know it.

Explaining forces you to retrieve the idea, connect it and put it in order. It's the most honest test there is, and anyone can take it.

This is the same idea behind the Feynman technique. Pause here. Everything in the product comes from this sentence.

Memorizing answers doesn't work. Understanding the idea does.

Why the CBC: huge volume, high stakes, and an exam that punishes almost-knowing. We read the real exams before building anything: the "versions" of an exam are the same questions shuffled, and ideas repeat across years. So we teach the idea, not the question.

Principles of the solution

Built on six principles

1

Training, not more content

UBA XXI already has videos and guides. We tell you which one to watch, down to the minute.

2

The unit is the idea

We teach claims ("According to Romero…"), not chapters. Questions, videos and your score all hang from them.

3

Confidence finds blind spots

After every answer: sure, unsure or guessed. Sure and wrong is a blind spot, and it goes first.

4

Explain, don't pick

Practice has no options. You explain, typed or spoken, and the tutor works out what you'd have marked.

5

Check against the source

The tutor grades against the author's text, not general knowledge. It never invents quotes or facts.

6

Readiness as a range

"Today you're between 5 and 6.5." Plus what would get you to a 7, the grade that exempts you from the final.

MapPreparePracticeFeedback→ the map updates, and the cycle starts again
Six decisions that make this different from a question bank. The most important are 3 and 4: we measure confidence to find blind spots, and in practice there are no options to pick from, so you can't guess or just recognize.

Why this path · AI enablement

AI that makes you think

We're not against AI. The same model that can write your essay can be the most patient, toughest tutor you've ever had. It depends on the role you give it.

AI as a shortcut

It answers for you

  • You ask, it answers
  • It does the thinking
  • Feels productive, nothing stays
  • You end up depending on it
AI as a coach

It makes you answer

  • You explain, it pushes back
  • It finds exactly what's missing
  • The effort stays where learning happens
  • You end up needing it less

AI enablement isn't teaching people to prompt. It's teaching them to learn with AI, and that skill carries over to everything else.

This is why we went this way. AI enablement usually means "use these tools to go faster". We think the deeper skill is using AI to understand faster without skipping the understanding. That works for students and for any team.

Mandatory training, onboarding, compliance: companies count completions. A course with a 98% completion rate tells you nothing about what people kept. The same question we ask students applies here: can you explain it?
